How to Build and Sell a Browser Extension: A Practical Guide for Beginners

November 26, 202610 min readDigital products

Most people think of digital products as e-books, templates and courses. Browser extensions rarely make the list, yet they can be some of the most useful digital products you can build. An extension lives inside the browser where people already work, and it can save someone a few minutes every day on a task they repeat constantly. If that task matters to their income, many will happily pay for the time saved.

This guide explains how a beginner can go from a small idea to a published, paid Chrome extension. It is practical and honest: many extensions earn nothing, and the ones that do usually solve one narrow problem for a clearly defined group of people.

Why extensions can be good products

  • They sit where the work happens. An extension appears on the exact website your customer uses all day: a marketplace, an email inbox, a social network or a CRM.
  • Small scope is enough. A tool that does one thing well can be valuable, so a solo creator can build it.
  • Built-in discovery. The Chrome Web Store has its own search, so people looking for a solution can find you.
  • Low running costs. Many extensions run entirely in the browser, without servers to maintain.

What real examples teach us

The most famous example is Honey, a coupon and price-tracking extension. In November 2019, PayPal agreed to acquire it for about $4 billion, and reported at the time that Honey had around 17 million monthly active users (TechCrunch, November 2019). That is an extreme outcome, not a realistic target, but it shows how much value can sit in a simple browser add-on.

A more relatable example is Closet Tools, built by solo founder Jordan O'Connor. It is a Chrome extension that automates repetitive sharing tasks for sellers on the reseller marketplace Poshmark. In interviews on the Indie Hackers podcast, its revenue was described as roughly $30,000 to $40,000 per month. The lesson is the niche: it serves one group of sellers on one platform, and it saves them time that directly affects their sales.

Not every story ends that way. Plenty of extensions gather thousands of free users and almost no paying customers. Users are not the same as revenue, which is why the next steps matter.

Find a narrow problem worth paying for

Look for tasks that are repetitive, happen on a specific website and are connected to money or time. Good places to look:

  • Marketplace sellers who repeat the same steps to list products, reply to buyers or track prices.
  • Marketers and social media managers who copy data between tools or prepare reports by hand.
  • Recruiters, real estate agents and salespeople who collect the same information from websites every day.
  • Arabic users who struggle with right-to-left text, mixed Arabic and English content or tools that were never designed for Arabic.

Read the one- to three-star reviews of existing extensions in your niche. Complaints about missing features, poor Arabic support or abandoned tools often point to an opportunity.

Validate before you build

Before writing code, check that people actually want the solution. Describe the extension in one sentence, list who it is for and what it saves them, and share it with potential users in communities where they gather. A simple landing page with an email sign-up, as described in our guide to lead magnets, can show whether people are interested enough to leave their details. If several people say they would pay, you have a reason to continue.

Building it, even if you are not a programmer

A Chrome extension is made of ordinary web files: a manifest.json file that describes it, HTML and CSS for any popup or settings page, and JavaScript for the logic. New extensions use Manifest V3, Chrome's current extension platform, which runs background logic in a service worker and asks you to declare the permissions you need.

AI assistants such as ChatGPT and Claude can help a beginner a lot here. Write a short specification first: what the extension does, on which websites, what the user clicks and what data it stores. Then ask the assistant to generate the files step by step, load the extension in Chrome's developer mode, test it and paste any errors back for fixes. Our guide to writing better AI prompts applies just as well to coding requests, and you can find more ready prompts on our free prompts page. Always test the result yourself and ask only for the permissions the extension truly needs.

Publishing on the Chrome Web Store

To publish, you register a Chrome Web Store developer account, which has historically required a small one-time registration fee; check the current amount on Google's official page when you sign up. You then prepare a clear listing: an icon, screenshots, a short and a detailed description, and a privacy policy that explains what data the extension handles. Every submission goes through a review, and extensions must have a single clear purpose and follow the store's policies, so read the current program policies before you submit.

How extensions make money

Google stopped offering its own Chrome Web Store payment system in 2020 and 2021, so developers now use third-party tools to charge. Common approaches include:

  • Freemium: a useful free version, with advanced features behind a paid plan.
  • Subscription: a monthly or yearly plan, which suits tools people use every day.
  • One-time license: a single payment to unlock the full version, often simpler for small tools.

Payments are usually handled through services such as Stripe, sometimes via tools built specifically for extensions. Whatever you choose, be transparent about pricing in your store listing, and never sell or misuse your users' data. For help choosing a price, see our guide to pricing products and services in the Gulf.

Getting your first users

Start with the store listing itself: a clear name, a description written around the words people search for, and screenshots that show the result. Then go where your users are: the forums, groups and social channels of the niche you serve. Short videos showing the extension saving time work well on social media, and every early user who gives feedback helps you improve. Ask happy users for honest reviews, and reply to every review, positive or negative.

Your next step: write down five repetitive tasks you or people you know do in the browser every week. Pick the one connected most directly to money or time, check the existing extensions and their reviews, and describe your solution in one sentence before you write any code.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need to know how to code to build an extension?

Basic web knowledge helps, but AI assistants can generate and explain much of the code for a simple extension. You still need to test it carefully, understand what it does and review the permissions it asks for.

How much does it cost to publish an extension?

The Chrome Web Store has historically charged a small one-time developer registration fee. Check the current amount on Google's official page. Many simple extensions have no other running costs.

Can I charge directly through the Chrome Web Store?

No. Google deprecated its Chrome Web Store payments system in 2020 and 2021, so developers use third-party payment providers and license checks instead.

Which browsers should I support?

Start with Chrome, which has the largest audience. Because Microsoft Edge is built on Chromium, many extensions can be published there with few changes, and Firefox supports a similar extension system.

How long does it take to earn money from an extension?

It varies widely and many extensions never earn much. Focusing on a narrow, valuable problem, validating demand first and listening to early users improves your chances.
