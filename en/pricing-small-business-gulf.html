How to Price Your Products and Services: A Practical Guide for Small Businesses in the Gulf

November 25, 20269 min readBusiness

Pricing is one of the most important decisions in any small business, and one of the most often guessed. Many owners copy a competitor's price, pick a number that "feels right", or keep prices low out of fear that customers will leave. The result is often a business that is busy but not profitable, or one that undervalues the work it does.

This guide walks through a practical way to set prices for products and services in the Gulf and the wider Arab market: start with your real costs, understand your margin and break-even point, then price according to value and market, and adjust with care.

Start with your real costs

You cannot set a healthy price without knowing what each sale really costs you. List two kinds of costs:

  • Variable costs that come with every unit sold: materials, packaging, delivery, payment fees, platform commissions and the time spent on each order or project.
  • Fixed costs you pay every month regardless of sales: rent, subscriptions, licences, salaries, phone and internet, and your own salary as the owner.

Many small businesses forget to include the owner's time and pay themselves nothing. If the business only works because you work for free, the prices are too low.

Margin and break-even

Your margin is what remains from the price after variable costs. That margin has to cover your fixed costs before you make any profit. The break-even point is the number of sales you need each month to cover fixed costs.

StepSimple example
Price per unit150 AED
Variable cost per unit60 AED (materials, packaging, delivery, fees)
Margin per unit90 AED
Monthly fixed costs9,000 AED
Break-even9,000 ÷ 90 = 100 units per month

The numbers above are only an illustration; use your own. If your break-even number is unrealistic for your current audience, you need to raise prices, reduce costs or increase average order value.

Three ways to set the price

There are many pricing strategies, but most small businesses combine three approaches:

  1. Cost-plus: add a margin on top of your costs. Simple and safe, but ignores what customers are willing to pay.
  2. Competitor-based: look at what similar businesses charge. Useful as a reference, but you do not know their costs or whether they are profitable.
  3. Value-based: price according to the value the customer receives, such as time saved, a better result or peace of mind. This often allows higher prices, especially for services and specialised products.

A practical approach is to use cost-plus as your minimum, competitors as a reference, and value to decide where you sit in the range.

Communicating value

Customers compare prices, but they buy value. Make the value visible: clear product photos and descriptions, what is included, guarantees, delivery times, before-and-after results and genuine reviews. Our guides to product descriptions that sell and customer reviews and social proof help with this. When customers understand what they get, price becomes one factor among many rather than the only one.

Pricing services and freelance work

For services, decide whether to charge by the hour, by the project or by a monthly retainer. Hourly rates are simple but reward slow work; project prices reward efficiency but need a clear scope; retainers give predictable income for ongoing work. Whatever you choose, write down exactly what is included, how many revisions, and what costs extra. Our guide to starting freelancing in the Gulf covers proposals and contracts in more detail.

Pricing digital products

Digital products such as templates, e-books and planners have almost no cost per sale, so the price depends mainly on value and positioning. Price in local currency, offer a small entry product and a premium bundle, and make the difference between them clear. For example, our Arabic marketing templates include single planners and bundles at different price points. Our guide to selling digital products in the Middle East explains more.

Packages, bundles and discounts

  • Good, better, best: three options at different levels help customers choose and often lead them to the middle option.
  • Bundles: combine related products to increase order value while giving a clear saving.
  • Discounts: use them with a reason and an end date, such as a seasonal campaign, and always calculate your margin after the discount and any extra advertising costs.

Frequent, unexplained discounts teach customers to wait for the next sale. Free delivery, a small gift or a bonus item can sometimes work better than cutting the price.

When and how to raise prices

Consider raising prices when costs increase, when demand is strong, or when your quality and reputation have clearly improved. Give existing customers notice, explain what has improved, and consider keeping the old price for loyal customers for a limited time. Test changes on new products or new customers first if you are unsure.

Frequently asked questions

Should I be the cheapest in my market?

Usually not. Competing only on price leaves little margin for quality, service and marketing, and a larger competitor can always go lower. Aim to be the best value for a specific customer instead.

Should prices end in 9 or 99?

It is a common practice, but it matters less than clear value and consistent pricing. Choose a style that fits your brand, whether that is rounded prices for premium products or 9-ending prices for everyday items.

How do I price a new product with no sales history?

Start from your costs and target margin, check competitor ranges, estimate the value to the customer, then test with early buyers and adjust based on their response.

Do I include VAT in my prices?

Tax rules differ by country and by business registration. Check the requirements that apply to you and show prices clearly, so customers know exactly what they will pay.

How often should I review my prices?

Review them at least once or twice a year, and whenever your costs, demand or product change significantly.
