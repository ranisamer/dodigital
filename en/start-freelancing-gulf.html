How to Start Freelancing in the Gulf: Find Clients, Set Your Rates and Get Paid

November 15, 20269 min readFreelancing

Freelancing has become a real career path across the Gulf and the wider Arab world. Businesses of every size need help with design, content, social media, video, translation, web development and marketing, and many of them prefer flexible, project-based support over a full-time hire. At the same time, freelancing is a business, not just a skill. The freelancers who last are the ones who choose a clear service, find clients consistently, price properly and manage their work like a small company.

This guide walks you through the first steps, with practical advice for freelancers working with clients in the region.

Choose one clear service to start

"I do design, writing, social media and websites" sounds flexible, but it makes you hard to remember and hard to recommend. Start with one service that a specific type of client needs and is willing to pay for. Good starting questions:

  • What do I already do well enough that someone would pay for it today?
  • Which kind of business needs this most? Restaurants, clinics, real estate agents, online stores, coaches?
  • What result does the client actually want? Not "posts" but more enquiries; not "a logo" but a brand that looks trustworthy.

A focused offer such as "Arabic and English product descriptions for online stores" or "monthly Instagram content for cafés" is easier to sell than a long list of skills. You can expand later once you have clients and reviews.

Stay legal from the beginning

Rules differ from country to country and change over time, so check the current requirements where you live and work. Several Gulf countries offer official routes for independent professionals, such as freelance permits or licences in the UAE and a freelance work document in Saudi Arabia. Working within the rules protects you, lets you issue proper invoices, and makes larger companies more comfortable hiring you.

Also check whether you need to register for VAT once your income passes the threshold in your country, and keep simple records of every invoice and expense from day one.

Build a simple portfolio, even before your first client

Clients buy proof. If you do not have client work yet, create it:

  1. Sample projects: redesign a local brand's social posts as a concept, write sample product pages, or edit a short video, and label them clearly as concept work.
  2. A one-page portfolio: a simple website, a PDF or a pinned highlight on Instagram showing three to six of your best pieces, what you did and the goal of each.
  3. Before and after: if your service improves something, show the difference.
  4. Clear contact details: a professional email and a WhatsApp Business number.

Quality beats quantity. Three strong, relevant samples are better than twenty random ones.

Where to find your first clients

Most first clients come from people who already know you, then from referrals. Work through these channels in order:

  • Your own network: tell friends, family and former colleagues exactly what you do and who you help. A clear sentence is easier to pass on.
  • Local businesses: look at businesses in your niche whose social media or website could clearly be better, and send a short, specific message with one useful idea.
  • LinkedIn and Instagram: post your work and short tips regularly. Many clients will check your profile before replying.
  • Freelance platforms: global marketplaces and regional platforms can bring early projects and reviews, but competition on price is high. Use them to build experience, not as your only source.
  • Agencies: many agencies outsource overflow work to reliable freelancers.
Tip: your outreach message should be about the client, not about you. Mention something specific about their business, suggest one improvement, and offer a quick call. Keep it short enough to read on a phone.

How to price your work

Underpricing is the most common beginner mistake. It attracts difficult clients, leaves no room for revisions, and is hard to raise later. Use a simple calculation as your starting point:

Minimum hourly rate = (monthly living costs + business costs + savings) ÷ realistic billable hours per month

Remember that not every working hour is billable: you also spend time on marketing, admin, learning and unpaid proposals. Once you know your minimum, choose a pricing model:

ModelBest forWatch out for
Per projectClear deliverables such as a logo or a websiteDefine the scope and number of revisions
Monthly retainerOngoing work such as social media or contentList exactly what is included each month
HourlyUnclear or advisory workTrack time honestly and share reports
PackagesRepeatable servicesOffer two or three clear tiers

Quote in the client's currency (AED, SAR, QAR and so on) and state whether your price includes VAT if you are registered.

Write proposals that win work

A good proposal is short and focused on the client's goal. A simple structure:

  1. Their goal in one or two sentences, in their words.
  2. Your approach: what you will do, step by step.
  3. Deliverables and timeline: exactly what they receive and when.
  4. Price and payment terms.
  5. Relevant samples and a clear next step, such as "reply to confirm and I will send the invoice".

Avoid long introductions about yourself. The client wants to know that you understood their problem.

Contracts, deposits and getting paid

Even a small project deserves a written agreement. It does not need to be complicated, but it should cover the scope, number of revisions, timeline, price, payment schedule, who owns the final files and what happens if the project is cancelled.

  • Ask for a deposit before starting, commonly a portion of the fee, especially with new clients.
  • Send proper invoices with your details, the client's details, the service, the amount and the due date.
  • Offer easy payment methods such as bank transfer or a payment link, and confirm receipt politely.
  • Deliver final files after full payment for one-off projects, and share watermarked or low-resolution previews before that.

Grow from freelancer to a small business

After your first few projects, focus on stability. Turn one-off clients into monthly retainers, ask happy clients for referrals and written reviews, and document your process so each project is faster than the last. Frequently asked questions

Do I need a licence to freelance in the Gulf?

In most Gulf countries you need some form of official permission to work independently, such as a freelance permit, licence or freelance document, depending on the country and your residency status. Requirements change, so check the official government channels in your country before you start invoicing clients.

How much should I charge as a beginner freelancer?

Start by calculating your minimum rate from your living costs, business costs and realistic billable hours, then compare it with what similar freelancers in your niche charge. Avoid competing only on price; position yourself on a clear service and a clear result instead.

Are freelance platforms worth it?

They can help you get your first projects and reviews, especially if you have no network yet. However, competition on price is strong and platforms take fees, so aim to build direct client relationships and referrals over time.

Should I ask clients for a deposit?

Yes, especially with new clients and one-off projects. A deposit confirms the client's commitment and protects your time. State the deposit and payment schedule clearly in your proposal and agreement.

Can I freelance in Arabic and English?

Yes, and bilingual skills are valuable in the region. Many businesses need content, design and communication in both languages. Show bilingual samples in your portfolio and make it clear which languages you work in.
